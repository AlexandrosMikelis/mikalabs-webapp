# Risk and uncertainty

## Concepts
- **Risk = probability × consequence** — a simplification for comparing defined classes of events. Requires a specified event type; the **reference class** choice can change the estimate.
- **Uncertainty**: probabilities can't be estimated. **Ignorance**: we don't know which scenarios can occur. **Deep uncertainty**: can't specify the full range of realistic outcomes (Marchau et al. 2019) → move from predict-then-act to robust, adaptive decision-making and precaution.
- **Type I error**: treating a false claim as true (false alarm; blocks useful systems). **Type II**: treating a true claim as false (missed danger). Decide which error is worse in context.
- Individually acceptable risks can aggregate into unacceptable ones.

## Risk acceptability — four moral factors
1. **Benefits outweigh risks** — benefits to whom, risks to whom?
2. **Risks and benefits are justly distributed** — are the vulnerable more exposed while others benefit? (prioritarian and contractualist tests)
3. **Informed consent / voluntariness** — voluntariness is a scale; consent is a threshold. Risks attached to an unavoidable service aren't truly voluntary.
4. **No better alternative** — less risky design, process, or no AI.
Size alone never settles acceptability.

## AI risk categories
Bias and discrimination · misuse and dual use (cyberattacks, weapons, surveillance) · disinformation, deepfakes and harm to democratic trust · job displacement in cognitive work · deskilling and enfeeblement · privacy and surveillance · environmental impact · autonomous weapons · loss of control over advanced systems.

## Advanced AI risk (for strategic questions)
- **Superintelligence**: intellect greatly exceeding human performance in virtually all domains (Bostrom 2014). **Singularity**: hypothetical point where AI surpasses humans and becomes uncontrollable.
- **Orthogonality thesis**: almost any level of intelligence is compatible with almost any final goal. **Instrumental convergence**: agents with different goals pursue similar means (self-preservation, resources, power). Together: danger without malice.
- **Chalmers**: AI → AI+ → AI++ (valid argument; premises contested).
- **Existential** risk (extinction or permanent loss of potential) vs **catastrophic** risk (large-scale harm, non-zero probability). Bad outcomes needn't be existential to warrant action.
- **Value alignment** and **longtermism** (heavy weight on far-future people) as responses; longtermism is contested because it can divert attention from present, identifiable harms.
- Practical stance: neither dismiss speculative harms nor ban everything conceivable; use sandboxing, staged deployment, access controls, monitoring and reversibility.

## Review questions
1. Which harms, to whom, how likely, how severe, how reversible?
2. Are probabilities genuinely estimable, or is this deep uncertainty?
3. Who benefits and who bears the risk? Did they consent? Could they opt out?
4. What lower-risk alternatives exist?
5. Which error type matters more here?
