# Sustainability and AI

## Definitions
- **Brundtland (1987)**: development that meets present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs.
- **Narrow** view: long-term environmental harms (emissions, resources). **Broad** view: environmental + social + economic sustainability. The broad view captures more harms but risks vagueness and can blur the tension between welfare and conservation (Kuhlman & Farrington 2010). State which you use.
- Grounds for valuing nature: anthropocentric (for humans, incl. future generations), sentientist (animal suffering), ecocentric (intrinsic value).

## Technology seen broadly
Beyond the artefact: users, institutions, rules (sociotechnical system), plus the network of **resources, infrastructure and waste** behind it (van Wynsberghe 2021). Crawford's *Atlas of AI*: mineral mining, data centres, and low-paid **micro-workers** who label data (Tubaro & Casilli 2019).

## AI for sustainability vs sustainability of AI
- **AI for sustainability**: AI applied to sustainability goals (grids, deforestation monitoring, efficiency).
- **Sustainability of AI**: the energy, water, materials, labour, surveillance and waste of building and running AI.
- **AI sustainability dilemma** (cf. van Wynsberghe 2021): AI can advance sustainability while its development and use undermine environmental and social sustainability. Evaluate both sides together; "green" application ≠ sustainable system.
- **Sustainable AI**: fostering ecological integrity and social justice across the whole AI lifecycle (idea → training → use → disposal → governance).
- **Data pollution**: harms of large-scale data processing analogous to pollution — tangible (environmental) and intangible (data spills, privacy violations, discrimination, erosion of the information ecosystem, model collapse from synthetic data).
- **Diffuse harms**: spread across people, places and time; individually small, collectively significant; can hit non-users.
- **Negative externalities**: costs imposed on others that don't appear on the balance sheet (grid strain, e-waste, disruption to institutions). Normative premise: enterprises should insure against or compensate for their externalities.

## Footprint
Impact points: **training**, **building and running data centres**, **inference/use** (repeated use at scale can exceed training). Drivers: compute and training duration, hardware efficiency, data-centre location and grid mix, offsets, plus water for cooling, chip manufacturing, hardware replacement, e-waste. Efficiency gains can increase total use (rebound). Offsets can create new injustice (e.g., 2025 Kenyan court halt of a carbon-offset project over community land taken without consent).
Policy levers: monitor emissions, climate-aware training guidelines, clean energy for data centres.

## Governance levels
- **Micro**: engineers and teams (model size, compute, provider choice, data minimisation).
- **Meso**: companies and institutions (standards, procurement, reporting, supplier and labour conditions).
- **Macro**: states, international bodies, social movements (regulation, treaties, measurement standards).
Fixing one level doesn't fix the others; ask whose interests the infrastructure embeds and who can contest them.

## Review questions
1. Does a smaller model, less frequent retraining, or a non-AI method meet the need?
2. What are the inference volumes and where does it run (grid mix, water stress)?
3. Who labels the data and under what conditions?
4. What externalities fall on non-users or communities?
5. Is the "for sustainability" benefit measured against the system's own footprint?
6. Which actions sit at micro, meso and macro level for us?
